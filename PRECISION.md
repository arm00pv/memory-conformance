# What comes back besides the answer

Generated by `precision.py`. Do not edit by hand.

Every number this repository publishes counts whether the right record arrived. None of them
counts what arrived **with** it. A top-three list carrying the answer and two unrelated records
scores exactly what a list carrying the answer alone scores, so a subject that answers more
questions by handing back more records is paid for the hit and never charged for the volume.
This is that charge, read from rows that were already on disk.

### Why anybody answers a question that has no answer

A question can be unanswerable and still share words with a record - a store holding a record
about GFM strikethrough has a neighbour for a question about GFM task lists. The rule that
certifies a question as unanswerable charges one that shares **half** its words with a single
record and warns about one that shares fewer, and here that distinction predicts who answers.

**6 of the 7 subjects that decide per question never answered a question whose words appear
nowhere in the store; the exception is `statecore-mcp 1.0.0` with 8.** The 7 subjects gave 51 answers on unanswerable questions between them;
43 of those landed on a question that shares a word with a record and 8 did not, and every one
of the 8 belongs to the exception named above, which hands back a record for every question on
corpus A and so has no threshold there to fail. For the rest, silence is not a judgement about
answerability: their matcher finds something or it finds nothing, and a question written in words
no record contains leaves it nothing to find.

Which changes what the twelve free points are. The score hands twelve of sixty to any subject that
returns nothing on the unanswerable questions, and items.py reported that every subject either
takes all twelve or none of them - a habit, not an ability. These are the same rows read at a
larger sample: the deciding subjects answer 15 of the 336 unanswerable questions in the four short
bands and 36 of the 98 in the long exam, because a band drawn from a real pool of packages cannot
avoid near neighbours. The points are not a habit and they are not a virtue. They are the price
of a clean band, and the same subject pays a different price on a different corpus.

And this reading changed the instrument that was measuring it. 8 of the exam's 98 unanswerable
questions have half or more of their words inside a single stored record. The rule that
certifies a question as unanswerable used to charge exactly that as **an unlabelled answer** -
a statement about where the answer is, made by a test that can only see which words are where.

Reading those questions showed the charge was wrong every time: `obliterator` is not a
path library, and `micromark-extension-gfm-strikethrough` is not a task-list extension.
What the rule could see was that **a word-overlap matcher will answer this question**, which is
a fact about the question's words. The subjects make the same equation the rule made, which is
why the positive control answered exactly the questions it charged. **A checker and a subject
that share one idea cannot check each other.**

So the validator now measures two things instead of confusing them. A question that shares most
of **each other's** words with one record is that record in disguise, and is still an error - that
is what a question nobody labelled looks like. A question that merely sits half inside a record
is a neighbour: printed with the fraction, never charged, because nothing that compares strings
can decide whether it should be answered. The exam now passes its own validator with no errors
and 8 neighbours printed, and the questions the control answered are in that list rather than in
the pin file.

## Junk around the answer

Every returned record that is not the expected one is either a near miss - same family, one word
off, which is what a top-three list is for - or noise. The split is mechanical: same name token of
four characters or more, or three or more shared words of five characters or more that are not
common to half the corpus. Both phrasings, all nine corpora, written half:

## Does the score charge for this

Every adapter on this repository's shelves whose verdict is published, read through the same lens
on corpus A. The score column is the ledger's own: top-three membership plus a correct silence.

| `control` | positive control | 37 of 60 | 42 of 60 | 0.32 | 63% |

Read down that table. The tidiest adapter on the shelf is `strict-match` - no
noise at all, every character it hands back part of an answer - and it scores 27 of 60. The
mutant built to answer anything, `greedy-match`, scores 43, six points above the
positive control, while carrying 1.86 noise records per correct answer and putting 28% of its
characters on an answer. **The score cannot tell those two apart as different kinds of thing.
The price column can.**

Prediction 4 named the wrong adapter, and the error is kept rather than edited out of it: the
ledger number it referred to - a matcher scoring 43 against the control's 37 - belongs to
`greedy-match`, not to `blind-search`, which scores 2. Checked on the
adapter the prediction named, it holds by a wider margin than it asked for: `blind-search`
answered every question it was asked - all 48 answerable ones and all 12 with no answer -
handed back 144 records, got 2 right, and 138 of the rest share neither a name nor three words
with what was asked for.

## What this does not settle

The near rule is a rule, and the split it draws is a definition rather than a discovery. It is
checked in one direction only - near pairs exist by construction in the supersession band and the
rule finds them - and nothing checks the other: a record called near may still be useless to the
caller. **The noise column is a count of records that share neither a name nor three distinguishing
words with the answer**, and not a count of wasted attention. The silence bill is the part of this
report that needs no rule at all, and it is the part to quote when only one number will be.

Nothing was re-run to write this. Every row was on disk already, so no subject was asked twice and
no new measurement can contradict an old one - but a defect in a corpus is inherited here as
everywhere else, and this reading found two. The first is a question two records answer equally:
two packages can carry the same description word for word, so a corpus built from real descriptions
asked the same words twice with a different expected record and graded one of the two answers
wrong. That is fixed - `questions.py` drops every copy, the four generated corpora were
rebuilt without them, and the 594 rows measured against the
dropped questions are excluded from every count here rather than re-run, because a question that two
records answer equally was not measuring the subject.

The second stands and is pinned: the exam's eight unanswerable questions whose words are half inside
a single record. Those are fair questions - the covering record is not the answer - and the charge
against them belongs to the validator's word-overlap rule, not to the corpus. A reader who wants the
validator to stop making it should read that as the next phase rather than a footnote.

Finally, no judgement of usefulness is made by any rule here, and none can be: deciding whether a
returned record HELPS a caller is not a counting question. It is the one place in this repository
where a model reading the record would beat any rule - so one was asked. `judge.py` puts the
plain question to a model, with no label and no corpus in the prompt, and this reading's labels are
its score:

- **45 of 54 pairs agree with the reader, kappa 0.67.** 0 record(s) this report calls noise the
  reader called relevant, and 9 it calls a near miss the reader calls unrelated.
  `JUDGE.md` prints the disagreements one by one. That is the first time the checker's
  judgement has been audited by anything other than itself.
