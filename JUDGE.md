# A second opinion on the labels

Generated by `judge.py`. Do not edit by hand.

Every label in the precision reading comes from a rule about words. This asks models the plain
question - would this record help the person who asked - one pair at a time, with no label and no
corpus name in the prompt, and reports how far each reader is from the rule, and how far the
readers are from each other.

Readers: `glm-5.3:cloud`, `deepseek-v4.1-flash:cloud`. Judgment counts: 54, 60. Cached in `results/judgments.json`
with the model in the key; re-running the report needs no model and no network.

On the **54 pairs both readers judged** they agree on 49 (91%), kappa **0.81**. That is the

## The same reader, asked twice

Every comparison above is between two different readers, which leaves open whether the
disagreement belongs to the task or to the model. A person is asked once; a model can be asked
twice, so the pairs each reader had already judged were put to it again. The second pass runs at
a non-zero temperature on purpose: a model at zero returns what it returned, and a number
produced that way measures the sampler being switched off rather than the reader being unsure.

| reader | pairs asked twice | said the same thing | kappa | relevant then not | not then relevant |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `deepseek-v4.1-flash:cloud` | 60 | 56 (93%) | 0.87 | 2 | 2 |
| `glm-5.3:cloud` | 53 | 53 (100%) | 1.00 | 0 | 0 |

The steadiest reader is `glm-5.3:cloud`, at kappa **1.00** with itself against
**0.81** with the other reader. That is the useful shape: two readers disagree with each
other more than either disagrees with itself, so the 0.81 between them is a ceiling on
the task and not on the sampling.

**`glm-5.3:cloud` answered every pair the same way twice.** A perfect repetition
under sampling is read two ways here and this report cannot choose between them: either the
judgement never came close to the boundary, or the endpoint was not sampling at all. The
other reader moved on 4 of 60 pairs under an identical request, which is the evidence that
asking twice really is asking twice rather than reading the same answer back.

What this cannot see: one model, one prompt and one training reading a pair twice is also one
way of being wrong twice. A consistent misreading - a reader that calls every near miss useful
- is invisible here, and it is exactly what the rule-against-reader table above is for. The two
tables are halves of one question and neither of them is the answer.

## A person, as the third labeller

Six pairs were put to the person who asked for this work - question and record, with no label, no
rule verdict and no reader verdict in front of them. Four were chosen BECAUSE the rule and both
readers disagree about them, so this is a probe and not a sample, and no kappa is computed over it.

| the rule says | pairs asked | the readers | the person |
|---|---|---|---|
| answer | 1 | agrees with the person on 1 of 1 | 1 said relevant |
| near | 4 | agrees with the person on 4 of 4 | 0 said relevant |
| noise | 1 | agrees with the person on 0 of 1 | 0 said relevant |

**On the 4 record(s) this rule excuses as near misses, the person called 4 unrelated.**
The readers called all 4 unrelated. The floor under the noise column is therefore no longer
two models' opinion of a rule: on the pairs where that judgement is questionable, a person
read the record and said the same thing.

The control held: on the record the corpus says answers the question, the person said relevant
(1 of 1), which is what a labeller paying attention says and what a contrarian does not.

## What this does not settle

Two readers at one temperature are two opinions, not a standard. Where they agree the rule has
something to be wrong about; where they split, the question has no agreed answer and the rule's
verdict there is a coin toss with a justification. Neither reader is calibrated against a person,
and the pairs to calibrate them are already drawn: `results/human-sample.md` is sixty of
them, at random, with a file beside it for the answers. `--import-human` takes
the answers and this report renders them; `--export-human=60` draws another
sixty, and now refuses while that file holds one. An hour of somebody's reading is the only
thing standing between this and a rate instead of a search.

The prompt is a judgement call of its own. It asks whether the record would help, not whether it
is the answer, because the corpus already says which record is the answer and asking again would
test nothing.
